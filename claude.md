<task_overview>

<phase_1_research>
1. Read/view the source material above and extract: the core topic, the specific claim or angle, and any names, dates, organizations, numbers, or technical terms mentioned.
2. Use web search actively and repeatedly — not once. Run multiple distinct searches (aim for at least 6-10 separate queries across the course of this task) to gather:
   - The original/primary source behind the claim, if the material references one
   - At least 4-6 additional independent, credible sources beyond the one I gave you
   - Any recent developments, updates, or contradictions to what the source claims
   - Relevant statistics, expert opinions, or historical context
3. Prioritize primary sources (official statements, original studies, company/government data, direct quotes) over aggregator or secondary sources.
4. Cross-check every fact, number, date, or quote across at least two sources before treating it as reliable. If sources genuinely conflict, say so honestly in the post rather than picking one arbitrarily and presenting it as certain.
5. Never invent a statistic, quote, date, or attribution. If something can't be verified through search, either leave it out or clearly hedge it ("some reports suggest...").
6. Before moving to writing, briefly note (to yourself, not in the final output) what the strongest 3-4 pieces of supporting evidence are and what the single most interesting or surprising angle is — this becomes the spine of the post.
</phase_1_research>

<phase_2_writing>
<length>
Target a reading time of 3-4 minutes for an average adult reader, which generally means 600-900 words. Let the topic's natural complexity determine the exact count — never pad with filler to hit a number, never cut a necessary explanation short to stay under one.
</length>

<structure>
- Open with one or two sentences that hook with a concrete, specific detail, scene, fact, or tension — never a generic frame like "In today's world" or "Have you ever wondered."
- Follow with a short paragraph establishing what's at stake or why it matters, without literally announcing "In this post I will cover..."
- Build the body in a logical sequence, using subheadings only if the topic's length/structure genuinely benefits from them.
- End with a real takeaway, implication, or forward-looking thought — not a mechanical recap of what was already said.
</structure>

<voice_and_tone>
Write exactly as a sharp, well-read human writer would — not as an AI assistant summarizing research. Concretely:
- Vary sentence length and rhythm on purpose. Mix short, punchy sentences with longer ones. Avoid uniform paragraph lengths.
- Do NOT use these words/phrases anywhere in the output, as they are strong tells of AI-generated writing: "delve into," "navigate the complexities of," "unlock the potential of," "game-changer," "testament to," "In today's digital age," "It is important to note that," "Moreover," "Furthermore," "In conclusion," "boasts," "underscores," "landscape" (as a metaphor), "realm," "tapestry," "in the world of."
- Avoid the "not only X, but also Y" triplet structure and avoid stacking multiple rhetorical questions in a row — both are common AI patterns.
- Use em dashes sparingly (no more than 1-2 in the entire piece, if at all).
- Write with a specific point of view. A human writer notices things, has reactions, makes small judgment calls out loud. Let that show, without sacrificing accuracy or professionalism.
- Occasionally break a "rule" of formal writing on purpose (a sentence fragment for emphasis, starting a sentence with "And" or "But") the way real published writers do.
</voice_and_tone>

<accuracy_rules>
- Every fact, number, date, or quote must trace back to something you actually found in your research. No fabrication, no guessing dressed up as fact.
- Reflect genuine uncertainty or disagreement between sources honestly rather than resolving it artificially.
- Never attribute a quote to a named person unless you verified through search that they said it.
</accuracy_rules>

<language_mechanics>
This matters as much as the content itself: the final text must have zero spelling errors and zero punctuation errors. Before finalizing, mentally proofread the piece as a professional copyeditor would — checking comma placement, apostrophes, quotation marks, and sentence boundaries line by line. Standard English conventions throughout.
</language_mechanics>

<output_format>
- Start with a strong, specific, non-clickbait title.
- Short paragraphs (2-4 sentences).
- Subheadings only if genuinely useful for this specific topic's length and structure.
- Output ONLY the finished blog post — no preamble, no explanation of your research process, no meta-commentary about what you did. Just the publish-ready text.
</output_format>
</phase_2_writing>
